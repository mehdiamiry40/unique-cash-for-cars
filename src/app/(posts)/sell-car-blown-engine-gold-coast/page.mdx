# Selling a car with a blown engine: repair it, replace it or sell it as is

<PostDetails slug="sell-car-blown-engine-gold-coast" />

Sell it as it is when the repair would cost close to what the car is worth once it runs again. Repair or replace the engine only when you have a firm written quote, the gap between that quote and the repaired value is comfortable, and you actually want to keep driving the car. Most people who decide to sell a car with a blown engine do so because that gap turns out to be thin.

The order matters. Get the fault diagnosed, get the repair priced, then compare it with what the car is worth in both states. Deciding before you have those numbers is how owners end up paying for a rebuild on a car they sell six months later for less than the rebuild cost.

**This guide is general information, not legal or mechanical advice.** Queensland's sale, registration and modification rules change. Check the current Transport and Main Roads guidance linked below for your circumstances, and rely on a qualified mechanic for the diagnosis.

## First, find out what has actually failed

"Blown" gets used for everything from a cracked piston to an engine that simply will not start. Those are very different bills. Common failures that end up described this way include:

- **Head gasket failure**, often after overheating: white exhaust smoke, coolant loss, oil that looks milky
- **Bearing failure**, sometimes called a spun bearing: a heavy knock that gets worse under load
- **A thrown connecting rod**, which can punch a hole in the block and is usually the end of that engine
- **Timing belt or chain failure** on an engine where the valves and pistons can collide
- **Hydrolock**, where water is drawn into the cylinders — a real risk on a flooded road or a car that sat through a storm
- **Seizure** from running without enough oil or coolant

A mechanic can usually narrow it down with a compression or leak-down test and a look at the oil and coolant. Pay for that before anything else. Occasionally the diagnosis turns up something far smaller than feared, and even when it does not, a written description of the fault is exactly what a buyer or a repairer will ask you for.

If the car has stopped where it failed — a shopping centre car park in Robina, the shoulder of the M1 — get it off the road first and diagnose it afterwards. Do not keep cranking or restarting an engine that has made a loud mechanical noise; that can turn a repairable fault into a destroyed one.

## Is a blown engine worth fixing? The repair-or-sell sum

The comparison is simpler than it looks. On one side: what the car would sell for with a healthy engine, minus the full cost of getting it there. On the other: what it is worth today, as it sits.

The full cost is where estimates go wrong. An engine replacement quote often covers the engine and fitting, but not the items a workshop finds once the old unit is out — the radiator that caused the overheating in the first place, hoses, the clutch on a manual, sensors, fluids. Ask what the quote excludes. Ask whether it could change after the engine is opened, and in which direction.

Then think about the rest of the car. A fresh engine does not make old suspension, a tired gearbox or a rusting sill any younger, and a car with high kilometres elsewhere can need the next big repair within a year or two. The case for fixing is strongest on a car that was otherwise in good order and that you would have kept for years anyway. It is weakest on an older car that was already heading toward its end.

If the car is still under finance, the payout figure belongs in this sum too. Our guide to [selling a car with finance owing](/selling-a-car-with-finance-owing-queensland) explains how the payout and the PPSR affect a sale.

## Replacing the engine: what Queensland allows

There are three broad routes: rebuild the existing engine, fit a reconditioned one, or fit a used engine from a wrecked car. They differ in cost, in how much is known about the replacement's history, and in what warranty comes with the work. Read the warranty terms before you choose, because they vary a great deal.

On the regulatory side, Transport and Main Roads treats a like-for-like engine replacement as a basic modification. Its [vehicle modifications guidance](https://www.tmr.qld.gov.au/Safety/Vehicle-standards-and-modifications/Vehicle-modifications.aspx) says basic modifications do not need approval or certification, provided they meet the requirements in the Queensland Road Vehicle Modification Handbook and the vehicle continues to meet the applicable standards. Fitting a different engine is another matter and can require certification by an approved person. Confirm which category your plan falls into before you buy anything, not after it is bolted in.

## What a buyer pays for when the engine is gone

A car with a blown engine is not a car with no value. What a buyer is paying for depends on the make, model, year and overall condition, how complete the car is, and how much demand there is for its salvageable parts. The gearbox, wheels and tyres, panels, glass, lights, seats and catalytic converter can all still count. The engine itself may retain some value as a core for rebuilding, depending on what failed.

Two things follow from that. Keep the car complete: every part you pull off to sell separately comes off the offer for the whole car, and a stripped shell is harder to load. And describe it honestly. An offer is made for the vehicle described, so a buyer who arrives to find a different car will price it again.

## Three ways to sell it

**A private sale, as is.** Some private buyers look for exactly this — a mechanic, a hobbyist, someone with the same model who needs a donor car. The catch is the paperwork. A private sale of a registered car generally needs a current safety certificate before the car is disposed of, and a car that cannot be driven to an inspection station is awkward to get certified. Private buyers also do not get a statutory warranty or consumer guarantees in Queensland, as the state's [guidance on buying used cars privately](https://www.qld.gov.au/law/your-rights/consumer-rights-complaints-and-scams/buying-products-and-services/buying-products/buying-a-car/buying-a-used-car/buying-privately) makes clear, which is part of why they offer less for a known fault. Put the fault in writing on the receipt either way.

**A trade to a licensed motor dealer.** Queensland does not require a safety certificate when a registered vehicle is traded to a licensed motor dealer. The important word is licensed: confirm the licence belongs to the entity actually buying the car. **An unregistered sale or a sale for parts.** An unregistered car can be sold without a safety certificate, and Queensland requires a registered car sold for parts to be de-registered first. If the car is still registered and will not be driven again, cancelling the registration also stops you paying for registration you no longer need and may bring an eligible refund. ## Getting a non-runner collected

Do not drive a car with a failed engine to its buyer, even a short way. It can seize, drop oil or coolant, or stop in the middle of an intersection. It has to be towed or winched onto a truck.

Tell whoever is collecting it that the engine does not run, and give them the practical details that decide the equipment: whether it rolls, whether the steering is locked, whether the keys are available, and where exactly it sits. A car in a Surfers Paradise apartment basement needs the clearance height and the parking level. A car on a steep driveway in Nerang or down the side of a Helensvale house needs to be reachable by a winch cable. Those details, given up front, are what stop a wasted trip.

## A short decision path

1. Get the fault diagnosed and written down.
2. Get a written repair or replacement quote, and ask what it excludes.
3. Compare the car's value running, minus that full cost, with its value as it is today.
4. If replacing the engine, confirm whether the new engine is like-for-like under TMR's modification rules.
5. If selling, choose the path that matches the registration status: private with a safety certificate, licensed dealer, or unregistered.
6. Keep the car complete and describe the fault honestly.
7. Arrange a tow or collection rather than driving it.
