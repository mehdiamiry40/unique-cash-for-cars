# Selling a car with a defect notice in QLD: repair it, clear it or sell it as it is

<PostDetails slug="sell-car-defect-notice-qld" />

Yes, you can sell it. Queensland's [defect notice guidance](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/vehicleinspection/randominspection/defect) says that after receiving a notice you can sell the vehicle to a licensed motor dealer or cancel its registration. What you cannot do is ignore the notice and carry on as if the car were an ordinary registered sale.

A **defect notice** is not a roadworthy failure. It is issued at the roadside, usually after a police officer or transport inspector stops the car for a random inspection, and it requires the owner to have the vehicle repaired so it is safe to drive. It can arrive with an on-the-spot fine as well. The choice you face is whether the repair is worth doing, and if not, which of the sale paths fits the car.

**This guide is general information, not legal advice.** Defect notices carry their own instructions, and the rules behind them change. Read your notice, and check the current TMR [defect notice](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/vehicleinspection/randominspection/defect) and [used-vehicle selling](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/rules/selling) pages before you act.

## Which defect notice do you have?

Queensland uses three kinds, and the type decides how much room you have.

- **Major defect notice:** the officer reasonably believes the vehicle poses an imminent and serious safety risk.
- **Minor defect notice:** the vehicle poses a safety risk, but not an imminent and serious one.
- **Self-clearing defect notice:** using the vehicle does not pose a safety risk, but the defect still has to be fixed.

If the defect is dangerous, the officer may also attach a **defect label** in a prominent place on the car. The label tells other enforcement officers the vehicle is not allowed on the road. It is an offence to remove it unless you are authorised to.

## What a defect notice in QLD lets you do with the car

The notice is the document that matters here, not a general rule. TMR says a dangerous vehicle can be ordered off the road immediately, so it cannot be driven away from where it was stopped. A tow truck or trailer is then the only lawful way to move it. For a less serious defect you may be allowed to drive to a specified place, such as home or a repair workshop, under conditions written on the notice.

Each notice also shows a date by which the defects must be repaired. If you need longer, TMR says to contact the officer or transport inspector who issued it about an extension. Ask before that date passes.

On the Gold Coast, the practical consequence of an off-the-road direction shows up quickly. A car stopped on the M1 shoulder or the Gold Coast Highway has to be moved by tow. One that made it home to a unit-block basement in Surfers Paradise or a narrow driveway in Labrador needs a collection plan that suits that access.

## Option 1: repair the car and clear the notice

Repair makes sense when the defect is modest and the car is otherwise sound: worn tyres, a broken light, a cracked windscreen, a leaking exhaust. It also keeps the ordinary private sale open later.

Clearing works in two steps:

1. Have the defects repaired.
2. Follow the clearance method printed on the notice. Some notices need an inspection, which may be done at an approved inspection station.

When the station records the inspection in TMR's [Inspection Certificates Online system](https://www.tmr.qld.gov.au/business-industry/accreditations/approved-inspection-station-scheme/inspection-certificates-online/ico-procedures/defect-vehicle-and-trailer-inspections), the clearance reaches TMR electronically and you do not have to visit a transport and motoring service centre. If the station cannot record it that way, the examiner signs the back of the hard-copy notice and hands it back to you.

A cleared defect notice is not a safety certificate. If you then sell the car privately with its registration, the normal rules apply, including a current certificate before disposal. Our guide to [selling a car without a roadworthy in Queensland](/sell-car-without-roadworthy-qld) covers that step and its exemptions.

## Option 2: sell to a licensed motor dealer

TMR names this path directly. The important word, as with the safety-certificate exemption, is **licensed**. A towing business, recycler or cash-for-cars buyer is not a licensed motor dealer just because it buys cars.

Check that the licence belongs to the legal entity actually acquiring the vehicle, and keep a signed receipt with the date, price, VIN and the defect-notice number. ## Option 3: cancel the registration and sell it unregistered

The second path TMR describes is cancellation. Once the car is unregistered, the defect notice no longer stands between you and a sale: TMR's selling guidance lists selling unregistered and selling to an auto-parts dismantler among the options for a defective vehicle, and a car sold for parts must be de-registered first.

Cancellation also deals with the plates and may produce a refund of unused registration. The steps are in our checklist for [cancelling Queensland registration](/cancel-car-registration-queensland-after-sale). Keep TMR's confirmation with the sale paperwork.

An unregistered car with an outstanding defect cannot simply be driven to its buyer. If you are tempted to move it under a permit, read our explanation of [when TMR will issue an unregistered vehicle permit](/unregistered-vehicle-permit-queensland) first; for a car the officer has ordered off the road, towing is the realistic option.

## How to decide: repair, clear or sell

Get a written repair quote that lists every defect on the notice, then put it next to what the car is realistically worth once it is fixed. The quote often looks different once a workshop has the car on a hoist. Brake and suspension defects on an older car can uncover rust or worn components the roadside check did not reach.

- **Repair and clear** when the quote is small compared with the car's value and you plan to keep driving it or sell it privately.
- **Sell to a licensed motor dealer** when you want the car gone without fixing it and would rather not cancel the registration yourself.
- **Cancel and sell unregistered** when the car is worth more in parts or salvage than on the road, or the repair would cost more than the car is worth.

## Checklist before you hand over the keys

1. Read the notice: its type, the conditions on driving, the repair-by date and the clearance method.
2. Leave any defect label where it is.
3. Decide on one path: repair and clear, licensed-dealer sale, or cancellation and unregistered sale.
4. If you rely on the dealer path, verify the buyer's motor-dealer licence.
5. Disclose the defect notice to the buyer and record its number on the receipt.
6. Complete the transfer or cancellation with TMR on time and keep the confirmation.
7. Arrange a tow or transporter for any car that cannot lawfully be driven.
