# Selling a car with LPG in Queensland: when you need a gas certificate

<PostDetails slug="selling-car-with-lpg-queensland" />

You usually need one, and it is a second document rather than a harder version of the first. Selling a car with LPG in Queensland means the gas installation is certified separately from the rest of the vehicle. A **gas certificate** comes from an authorised gas installer and covers the gas system; a **safety certificate** comes from an approved inspection station and covers brakes, tyres, steering, lights and the rest. Neither one stands in for the other.

Which of them you actually need depends on the sale path, not on whether the gas system still works. A car whose cylinder is years out of date can still be sold — just not always down the route the owner had in mind.

**This guide is general information, not legal advice.** Gas and registration rules change. Check TMR's current [used-vehicle selling rules](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/rules/selling) and the Queensland [gas work and devices guidance](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/mining-energy-water/resources/safety-health/petroleum-gas/gas-work-devices) before you complete a sale.

## Selling a car with LPG in Queensland: the four sale paths

- **Registered car sold privately:** a gas certificate for the gas system and a safety certificate for the vehicle. TMR requires the gas certificate's issue date to be no more than three months old when the registration is transferred.
- **Registered car traded to a licensed motor dealer:** no safety certificate is required for that trade, but the gas certificate sits under the gas rules rather than the safety-certificate exemption. Confirm it with TMR for your transaction rather than assuming one exemption carries the other.
- **Unregistered car:** the gas certificate requirement is written around the registration transfer. Where the car is sold unregistered, there is no transfer for it to attach to.
- **Car being dismantled:** a registered vehicle sold for parts has to be de-registered first, and the cylinder is dealt with as a pressure vessel by whoever processes the car.

The three-month window is the detail that catches people out. A gas certificate obtained early in a slow private sale can expire before the buyer gets to the transfer, and then it has to be done again.

## A gas certificate is not a roadworthy

The two inspections are carried out by different people under different rules. An approved inspection station issues the [Queensland safety certificate](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/registration/roadworthy); it does not certify the gas installation, and booking a safety inspection will not get you a gas certificate as a by-product. Our guide to [selling without a roadworthy in Queensland](/sell-car-without-roadworthy-qld) covers when the safety certificate itself is and is not required.

Gas work on a vehicle — installing, altering, repairing, testing or certifying the system — has to be done by a person or business holding the relevant gas work authorisation. That is what makes the certificate worth anything, and it is why a general mechanic who is happy to service the car may still send you elsewhere for the gas side.

## Check the date stamped on the cylinder first

Automotive LPG cylinders are stamped with a test date. Refuelling outlets will not fill a cylinder past it, so an expired cylinder is not a paperwork problem you can deal with later — it is the point at which a dedicated gas car stops being refuellable at all.

The options are the ordinary ones: have the cylinder retested and restamped, replace it, or stop using the gas system. Each is a real cost, and on an older car it is often the cost that decides the whole question. Work out what the retest or replacement is quoted at, then compare it against what the car is worth with and without a working gas system before you commit to either.

Dual-fuel and dedicated vehicles land in very different places here. A **dual-fuel** car with an expired cylinder still drives on petrol, so the owner has time and choices. A **dedicated** gas car with an expired cylinder cannot be refuelled, which means it has to be moved on the fuel already in the tank or transported — a detail worth sorting out before you arrange anything, not on the day.

## Removing the gas system instead

Taking the system off a dual-fuel car and selling it as a petrol vehicle is a legitimate path, and sometimes the cheapest one. It has to be done by someone with the relevant gas work authorisation, not in the driveway.

It also changes details recorded against the vehicle. Check TMR's current [vehicle modification requirements](https://www.tmr.qld.gov.au/safety/vehicle-standards-and-safety/vehicle-modifications) for what has to be notified and how the gas compliance plate is dealt with, and confirm it before the work is booked rather than after. If the registration is going to be cancelled anyway because the car is leaving the road, the removal may be effort spent on a problem that was about to stop mattering — our [registration cancellation checklist](/cancel-car-registration-queensland-after-sale) sets out that path.

## What the gas system is worth at the end of the car's life

On a car that someone intends to keep driving, a working, in-date system is a genuine selling point, and the certificate is part of what makes it saleable.

On a car headed for dismantling it works differently. The value comes from the same things it always does — make, model, year, condition, completeness and how much demand there is for the salvageable parts. The gas installation is not a separate windfall on top of that; the cylinder is handled as a pressure vessel that has to be made safe before the shell is processed. What does matter is completeness. Stripping usable components off the car before it is collected generally leaves less recoverable value in it, not more. Our guide to [what a scrap car is worth on the Gold Coast](/how-much-is-my-scrap-car-worth-gold-coast) goes through the factors that actually move the figure.

## Telling the buyer it is a gas car

Say so when you first describe the vehicle. An LPG cylinder changes how the car is handled, and a buyer who finds out on arrival has to re-plan the job on the spot.

Three things are worth mentioning up front: whether the car is dual-fuel or dedicated, whether the cylinder is in date, and how much fuel is in it. If the car cannot be driven — expired cylinder on a dedicated system, an empty tank, or anything else — it needs transport rather than a trip to the buyer. Gold Coast access adds its own wrinkles: an apartment basement at Surfers Paradise has a clearance limit the recovery truck has to fit under, a Nerang acreage driveway may be too soft for a tilt tray, and a car parked kerbside on a narrow Labrador street needs room to be loaded. None of that is unusual, but all of it is easier to plan before the truck leaves than after it arrives.

## Before you accept an offer

Selling a car with LPG in Queensland goes wrong in the same handful of places. Work through these before you agree to anything.

1. Confirm whether the vehicle is dual-fuel or dedicated gas.
2. Read the test date stamped on the cylinder and note whether it has passed.
3. Decide the sale path: registered private transfer, licensed-dealer trade, unregistered sale or dismantling.
4. Obtain the gas certificate from an authorised gas installer if the path involves a registration transfer, and time it so the issue date is still inside the three-month window at transfer.
5. Obtain the safety certificate, or cancel the registration, according to that same path. Our [registration transfer guide](/transferring-car-registration-in-queensland) covers who lodges what.
6. Tell the buyer the fuel system details and the cylinder's status before anything is booked.
7. Arrange transport rather than driving a vehicle you cannot safely refuel.
