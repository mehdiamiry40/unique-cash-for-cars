# 5 luxury electric cars to compare in Australia in 2026

> **Updated 29 July 2026.** The original version of this article covered the
> much smaller Australian market of 2020. It has been completely rewritten
> around vehicles and manufacturer specifications current in Australia in 2026.

Calling any new car “eco-friendly” needs a qualification. A battery-electric
car has no tailpipe emissions, but manufacturing a large vehicle and its battery
still consumes materials and energy. The electricity used for charging matters,
too. Keeping a sound car for longer can have a smaller footprint than replacing
it early simply to buy something labelled green.

With that said, a fully electric vehicle can substantially reduce the fuel use
and local exhaust emissions attached to everyday driving. The five cars below
are not ranked from one to five. Each is the strongest fit for a different kind
of luxury buyer.

All range figures are manufacturer-quoted laboratory results. Temperature,
speed, hills, tyres, air conditioning, load and driving style all change what
you will see on the road.

## 1. Polestar 4 — the long-range design choice

The Polestar 4 is the unconventional one. It combines a coupé roofline with SUV
space and replaces the rear window with a roof-mounted camera and digital
mirror. That decision will either feel clever or unnecessarily complicated, so
it is something to test at night and in wet weather rather than accept from a
brochure.

The rear-motor version is the range leader. [Polestar Australia quotes up to
620 km WLTP](https://www.polestar.com/au/polestar-4-models/polestar-4-coupe/specifications/),
with 200 kW maximum DC charging and a claimed 10–80% charge in 30 minutes under
suitable conditions. The dual-motor version trades some range for much stronger
performance.

**Best suited to:** buyers who want long official range, a modern interior and
something less familiar than the established German badges.

**Check before buying:** whether the camera-only rear view suits every driver in
the household, and whether your regular charging stops can deliver enough power
to make use of the car's 200 kW peak.

## 2. BMW iX xDrive45 — the quiet long-distance luxury SUV

The BMW iX is the least conventional-looking BMW and one of the more
conventional luxury experiences once you are inside it. Its strengths are space,
ride comfort and the sense that the electric drivetrain was designed into the
vehicle rather than substituted for an engine late in development.

[BMW Australia lists up to 616 km of range for the iX
xDrive45](https://www.bmw.com.au/content/bmw/marketAU/bmw_com/en/models/bmw-i/ix/bmw-ix.html).
It quotes 175 kW DC charging and about 34 minutes for 10–80% in suitable
conditions. That peak is lower than several newer 800-volt competitors, but the
official range means many trips need fewer charging stops in the first place.

**Best suited to:** families wanting a roomy five-seat luxury SUV for motorway
and regional travel.

**Check before buying:** the actual wheel-and-tyre specification. Large optional
wheels can affect efficiency, ride quality, replacement cost and the distance a
car travels between charges.

## 3. Porsche Macan Electric — the driver's choice

The electric Macan is for someone who still wants the steering, body control and
driving position to matter. It is a practical SUV, but Porsche's reason for
building it is not simply efficiency.

The Australian range includes rear-wheel-drive and all-wheel-drive variants.
[Porsche Australia's current Macan range](https://www.porsche.com/australia/models/macan/)
quotes up to 529 km WLTP for the rear-wheel-drive Macan Electric, while the
quicker Macan 4, 4S, GTS and Turbo trade range and cost against performance.
The model's 800-volt electrical architecture is designed for high-power rapid
charging when a compatible charger is available.

**Best suited to:** drivers who want an EV that still feels deliberately sporty,
not merely very fast in a straight line.

**Check before buying:** the configured price rather than the headline price.
Wheels, suspension, driver-assistance features and cabin options can materially
change both the cost and character of a Porsche.

## 4. Volvo EX90 — the seven-seat family choice

The EX90 is the only true seven-seater on this list. It focuses on family space,
a quiet cabin and driver-support technology rather than trying to disguise its
size.

[Volvo Australia currently estimates 521 km of
range](https://www.volvocars.com/au/cars/ex90-electric/) and around 24 minutes
for a 10–80% charge at a suitably powerful DC station. Its navigation and range
tools can pre-condition the battery before a planned charging stop, which is
particularly useful on a long trip.

**Best suited to:** households that genuinely use a third row and want a
fully-electric alternative to a large premium petrol or plug-in-hybrid SUV.

**Check before buying:** third-row space with the passengers who will use it,
boot room with all seven seats raised, and whether a vehicle of this size is
necessary for most trips. More mass and battery capacity still require more
energy and material.

## 5. Lexus RZ 500e — the straightforward luxury transition

The 2026 RZ 500e is a substantial update rather than a badge change. It pairs a
74.7 kWh battery with dual-motor all-wheel drive and a more familiar luxury-car
experience than the screen-led minimalism of some EVs.

[Lexus Australia quotes up to 460 km
WLTP](https://www.lexus.com.au/news/2026/05/lexus-expands-rz-line-up-with-new-performance-model)
for the RZ 500e, 22 kW AC charging and 150 kW maximum DC charging. The official
range is shorter than the Polestar and BMW, but it may be more than enough for a
car charged at home every night.

**Best suited to:** buyers who value a conventional dealer and ownership
experience and do not need the longest possible touring range.

**Check before buying:** the difference between the RZ 500e Luxury, Sports
Luxury and 550e F Sport. The F Sport's steer-by-wire controls and virtual gear
shifts are not simply cosmetic upgrades.

## How to choose without being distracted by one number

Maximum range is useful, but it is not the whole buying decision. Work through
these questions in order:

1. **Can you charge where the car sleeps?** A modest-range EV charged at home is
   usually easier to live with than a long-range EV dependent on public
   chargers.
2. **What is the longest trip you make regularly?** Plan that route using the
   chargers you would actually stop at, not a theoretical maximum.
3. **How quickly does it charge across the session?** Peak kW is only a moment.
   The time from 10–80% is generally more useful.
4. **What does the exact specification do to range?** Larger wheels, performance
   tyres and extra mass can move the number.
5. **What will insurance and tyres cost?** Heavy, powerful luxury EVs can be
   inexpensive to charge and expensive to insure or re-tyre.
6. **How long is the battery warranty, and does it transfer?** This matters
   especially for a demonstrator or used vehicle.

## If you are buying used

Ask for a battery-health report where the manufacturer or an independent EV
specialist can provide one. Check that both charging cables are present, confirm
the remaining vehicle and battery warranty in writing, and test AC and DC
charging rather than assuming the socket works because the dashboard shows no
warning.

Also inspect the ordinary-car items: uneven tyre wear, accident repairs,
coolant leaks, suspension noise and a complete service history. An electric
drivetrain removes many engine-maintenance items; it does not remove tyres,
brakes, suspension, air conditioning or collision damage.
