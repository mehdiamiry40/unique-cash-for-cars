# Selling a car with a defect notice in Queensland: which path clears it

<PostDetails slug="selling-car-defect-notice-queensland" />

Yes, you can, but the notice does not go away because the car changes hands. Queensland Transport and Main Roads treats repair as the standard way to deal with a defect notice, and lists two alternatives for an owner who does not want to fix the car: **sell it to a licensed motor dealer** or **cancel the registration**. Selling a car with a defect notice in Queensland is really a choice between those three paths, made before the time on the notice runs out.

The type of notice decides how much room you have. Some cars can still be driven to a workshop or an inspection; others carry a label that keeps them off the road entirely.

**This guide is general information, not legal advice.** Enforcement rules and forms change. Your notice is the authority for your car: it states the defect, what must be done and how to clear it. Check TMR's current [defect notice guidance](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/vehicleinspection/randominspection/defect) before you rely on any summary, including this one.

## Which defect notice do you have?

A police officer or transport inspector can issue a defect notice to a vehicle's owner or driver. Queensland uses three kinds, graded by how much risk the officer believes the car poses on the road:

- **Major defect notice:** using the vehicle on a road poses an imminent and serious safety risk.
- **Minor defect notice:** it poses a safety risk, but not an imminent and serious one.
- **Self-clearing defect notice:** the officer believes it does not pose a safety risk.

The kind is printed on the notice, along with the defects found and the action required. Read the conditions section closely. Depending on how unsafe the car is, the officer decides whether it may be driven before the notice is cleared, and on what terms.

## Can the car still be driven?

Only as the notice allows. Where a defect is considered dangerous, the officer may also attach a **defect label** somewhere prominent on the vehicle. The label tells other enforcement officers that the car is not allowed to be driven on the road.

Removing that label yourself is an offence, and it can attract an on-the-spot fine. Selling the car does not change that, and nor does a buyer who is happy to "just take it as is". A labelled car parked in a Helensvale driveway or a Surfers Paradise basement has to leave on a tow truck or a tilt tray, not under its own power. The same goes for the buyer who offers to drive it home up the M1.

If the car is unregistered as well as defective, an unregistered vehicle permit does not solve the problem either. Our guide to [unregistered vehicle permits in Queensland](/unregistered-vehicle-permit-queensland) explains why a car that cannot be driven safely is usually better transported.

## Path one: repair the defects and clear the notice

This is the path the notice is written for. Once the car is repaired, follow the clearance instructions on the notice. Some notices can be cleared through an **approved inspection station**: the station [records the defect inspection electronically](https://www.tmr.qld.gov.au/business-industry/accreditations/approved-inspection-station-scheme/inspection-certificates-online/ico-procedures/defect-vehicle-and-trailer-inspections), which means you do not need to visit a transport and motoring service centre such as Southport or Bundall to clear it. Others name a different clearance method, so do what your notice says rather than what a friend's said.

Repairing makes the most sense when the defect is modest and the car is otherwise worth keeping or selling privately. A worn tyre, a cracked windscreen or a failed light is rarely a reason to give up on a car. A badly corroded sill, a failed brake system on an older car, or a defect discovered alongside a tired engine can be a different calculation. Get a written quote for the repair, then weigh it against what the car is realistically worth once it is fixed. Our guide on whether a [blown engine is worth fixing](/blown-engine-repair-or-sell) walks through the same repair-or-sell arithmetic.

## Path two: sell the car to a licensed motor dealer

TMR lists selling to a licensed motor dealer as an alternative to repairing the defects. The word that matters is **licensed**. A tow truck, a website or a business describing itself as a car buyer does not by itself establish that the entity acquiring your car holds a Queensland motor-dealer licence.

Verify the licence belongs to the legal entity whose name will appear on the receipt. Tell any dealer about the defect notice and its type before they quote. The notice changes how the car can be moved and what work it needs, and a quote made without knowing about it is not a quote for your car.

## Path three: cancel the registration

TMR's other listed alternative is cancelling the vehicle's registration. Once the car is unregistered it can be sold without a safety certificate, but it still cannot simply be driven on public roads, and a car with a defect label still has to be transported.

Cancelling also matters if the car is going for parts or scrap. TMR's [used-vehicle selling guidance](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/rules/selling) says a registered vehicle sold for parts must be de-registered first. Use our checklist for [cancelling Queensland registration after a sale](/cancel-car-registration-queensland-after-sale) to handle the plates and any refund in the right order, and keep TMR's confirmation with the signed receipt.

## What about a normal private sale?

A defect notice does not create a new kind of sale. For an ordinary private sale of a registered light vehicle, the seller generally needs a current safety certificate before disposal. A car with an outstanding safety defect is unlikely to pass that inspection, so in practice a private registered sale means repairing first. Our guide to [selling without a roadworthy in Queensland](/sell-car-without-roadworthy-qld) sets out when the certificate is and is not required.

Whatever path you choose, tell the buyer about the notice in writing. A buyer who finds a defect label or an enforcement record after the sale is a dispute waiting to happen, and the defect history is not something you can lawfully hide by peeling off a sticker.

## What happens if you do nothing

Failing to comply with a defect notice is an offence. Queensland's guidance also says it may result in action to cancel the vehicle's registration. Leaving a defective car on the driveway and hoping the notice is forgotten rarely ends well: the obligation stays with the owner, and the car keeps losing value while it sits.

If the car is not worth repairing, choose a dealer sale or a cancellation while you still have time to do it in an orderly way.

## Checklist before you accept an offer

1. Read the notice: its type, the defects listed, any driving conditions and the clearance method.
2. Decide whether you are repairing, selling to a licensed motor dealer or cancelling the registration.
3. If repairing, get a written quote and compare it with the car's realistic value.
4. If selling to a dealer, verify the motor-dealer licence belongs to the entity buying the car.
5. If cancelling, do it before an unregistered sale or a sale for parts, and keep TMR's confirmation.
6. Leave any defect label in place and arrange transport if the notice does not allow driving.
7. Give the buyer the notice details in writing and record the VIN, date, price and both parties in a signed receipt.
