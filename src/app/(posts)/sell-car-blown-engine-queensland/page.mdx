# Selling a car with a blown engine: repair, replace or sell as is

<PostDetails slug="sell-car-blown-engine-queensland" />

Yes, you can sell a car with a blown engine, and often it is the sensible call. The decision comes down to one comparison: what the car would realistically be worth running, against the full written cost of getting it running again. If the gap is thin or negative, selling it as it sits usually leaves you better off than funding a new engine in an old car.

The engine failure does not change the Queensland sale rules. Registration status and the buyer still decide whether you need a safety certificate, and a car that cannot be driven still has to reach the buyer safely.

**This guide is general information, not legal advice.** Rules and forms change. Check the current [Queensland Transport and Main Roads used-vehicle selling guidance](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/rules/selling) for your circumstances before completing a sale.

## Confirm what has actually failed

"Blown" covers a wide range of faults, and the repair bill tracks the fault, not the word. Before you price anything, get a written diagnosis from a mechanic that says which of these you are dealing with:

- **Head gasket or cylinder head damage**, often after overheating. Sometimes repairable in the car.
- **Seized engine**, from oil starvation or a failed bearing. The engine will not turn over by hand.
- **Mechanical breakage**, such as a snapped timing belt in an interference engine, or a connecting rod through the block.
- **Hydrolock**, where water drawn into the intake has bent internal parts. Relevant after driving through floodwater on the Gold Coast's low-lying roads.

A compression or leak-down test is a small cost next to either an engine replacement or selling a repairable car for less than it was worth. Keep the written result. It is useful whichever way you go, because a buyer can price a described fault more confidently than a mystery.

## Repair, replace or sell: which case are you in?

- **Minor, diagnosed fault on a car worth keeping:** repair it, then sell or keep it as a running car.
- **Engine beyond repair, rest of the car in good order and in demand:** a replacement engine may be worth pricing properly.
- **Engine gone and the car has other ageing problems:** tyres, rust, transmission, suspension or a registration about to lapse all add to the real cost. Selling as is usually wins.
- **Failure after a flood or collision:** talk to your insurer before anything else. The claim may decide what happens to the car.
- **Finance still owing:** the lender's interest has to be cleared whichever option you choose.

Most comprehensive policies exclude mechanical breakdown and wear, so do not assume a blown engine is claimable. Damage from an insured event, such as a flood, can be different. Read your product disclosure statement and ask the insurer before you authorise repairs or sell the car, because selling it before the insurer has assessed it can complicate the claim.

## Replacing the engine: the rules and the real cost

Before you commit, understand how Queensland classifies the work. TMR treats a **like-for-like engine replacement** as a basic modification: it does not need approval or certification by an approved person, as long as the vehicle still meets the applicable vehicle standards. An **engine upgrade** is a complex modification, which must follow a Queensland-approved modification code and be certified by an approved person. The detail is in TMR's [vehicle modification guidance](https://www.tmr.qld.gov.au/safety/vehicle-standards-and-safety/vehicle-modifications). If the replacement is not identical to the original, confirm the category before the engine goes in, not after.

Check whether your insurance policy requires you to disclose an engine change as well. That is a matter for the policy rather than TMR, and an undisclosed change can affect a later claim.

Then compare the whole job, not the headline part price:

1. The replacement engine, whether new, reconditioned or used, and any warranty that comes with it.
2. Labour, plus the ancillary parts that are usually replaced at the same time: gaskets, hoses, sensors, fluids.
3. Whatever caused the failure. A cooling fault that cooked the first engine will cook the second.
4. The other work the car needs in the next year or two anyway.

Set that total against what the same make, model and year sells for running, in similar condition. A used engine of unknown history can be a sound choice on a common model. It can also mean paying twice.

## Selling a car with a blown engine as is

A failed engine does not create its own sale category. The same paths apply as for any other defective vehicle, and TMR lists them: repair the defects, sell to an auto dealer, sell to an auto-parts dismantler, or sell the vehicle unregistered.

- **Registered, sold privately:** a current safety certificate is generally required before disposal. For a car that will not run, that is rarely practical.
- **Registered, traded to a licensed motor dealer:** no safety certificate is required for that trade. Verify that the licence belongs to the legal entity actually acquiring the car.
- **Sold for parts:** a registered vehicle must be de-registered first.
- **Unregistered:** it can be sold without a safety certificate, with a signed record of the date, the make and model, and the VIN, chassis or engine number.

## What a non-running car is still worth

A buyer of a non-runner is paying for everything except the engine: the transmission, body panels, glass, wheels, interior, electronics and the catalytic converter, plus the metal left at the end. The engine itself may still have some value as a core or for parts, depending on the failure.

That is why the same fault can mean very different offers on two cars. Make, model, year, overall condition, completeness and parts demand drive the number, not the fact that it will not start. Our guide to [what a scrap car is worth](/how-much-is-my-scrap-car-worth-gold-coast) explains how those factors combine.

Two things reliably reduce what you are offered. Stripping parts before the sale takes value out of the car, and describing the fault vaguely means any offer has to be confirmed once the car is seen. Keep it complete and describe it accurately.

## Moving a car that will not start

Do not try to nurse a car with a failed engine to a buyer. A seized or broken engine can lock up at speed, and one that is losing coolant or oil can fail completely a few kilometres down the M1. Arrange transport instead.

Whoever collects it needs to know:

- whether the car rolls, steers and brakes, and whether the gearbox will select neutral
- where it is: a driveway, the street, a workshop yard, or an apartment basement in Surfers Paradise or Southport with a clearance bar
- anything blocking it in, and whether the keys are available

A workshop in Nerang or Molendinar holding a car after a failed diagnosis may charge storage, so confirm the workshop's release arrangements before booking collection.

## Before you accept an offer

1. Get a written diagnosis and keep it with the car's paperwork.
2. Check your insurance position if the failure followed a flood or collision.
3. Obtain a finance payout letter if money is owing.
4. Decide whether the sale is a dealer trade, an unregistered sale or a parts sale, and prepare the registration step that path needs.
5. Keep the car complete, and describe the fault and the location accurately.
6. Record the buyer, seller, date, amount and VIN in a signed receipt, and complete the transfer or cancellation on time.
