# Dead hybrid battery: repair it, replace it or sell the car as it is

<PostDetails slug="dead-hybrid-battery-repair-or-sell" />

Often repairing is the right call, but not always. A dead hybrid battery is one expensive part in a car that may otherwise be sound. Three things decide it, and you can usually have all three within a week: whether a warranty or the consumer guarantees still cover the fault, a written repair quote, and what the car is worth with and without a working battery.

Work through them in that order. Selling first can cost you a claim you did not know you had, and paying for a new pack first can put more into an old car than it will ever be worth.

**This guide is general information, not legal or mechanical advice.** Warranty terms, consumer-law remedies and Queensland registration rules change. Check the current manufacturer terms, the [ACCC's warranty guidance](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/warranties) and [TMR's safety-certificate rules](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/registration/roadworthy) for your circumstances.

## Which situation are you in?

- **The car is still inside the manufacturer's battery warranty:** book it in with a franchised dealer before anyone else touches the pack.
- **The warranty has expired, but the car is fairly young or has done low kilometres:** the consumer guarantees may still apply if you bought it from a business. Ask before you pay for a repair.
- **The car is older, the fault is confirmed and no claim applies:** get a written quote and compare it with the car's value.
- **The battery is one of several faults:** a worn gearbox, rust, an overdue timing service or a failed roadworthy item changes the maths. Selling is more often the sensible answer.
- **The car has also been in an accident or a flood:** the insurer's decision comes first. Our guide to [statutory and repairable write-offs](/statutory-vs-repairable-write-off-queensland) explains what each classification allows.

## First, confirm it really is a dead hybrid battery

A hybrid system warning and a car that will not go into READY mode do not prove the main battery has failed. Before you decide anything based on the cost of a new pack, have the fault read with a diagnostic tool by someone who works on hybrids.

Two cheaper faults commonly look like it:

- **A flat 12-volt auxiliary battery.** Hybrids still carry an ordinary 12-volt battery that powers up the system. If it is flat, the car will not start, even when the high-voltage battery is healthy.
- **A blocked battery cooling fan or intake.** The pack is cooled by air drawn from the cabin. A clogged intake or filter can overheat it and trigger warnings well before the cells are worn out.

The high-voltage pack itself is not a home project. It runs at a voltage that can seriously injure you, so leave removal and testing to a workshop with hybrid training.

## Check the warranty and consumer guarantees before you pay

Hybrid battery cover is often longer than the car's general warranty. Toyota, for example, says its [hybrid battery warranty](https://www.toyota.com.au/owners/warranty) can extend to 10 years with an annual hybrid health check done as part of routine servicing, but the extension does not cover commercial use such as rideshare. Other makes set their own terms, so find the warranty booklet or ask a franchised dealer with the VIN.

If the warranty has run out, you may still have a remedy. The **consumer guarantees** in the Australian Consumer Law are separate from any warranty. According to the ACCC, they can still apply after a warranty expires when a product was not reasonably durable for what it cost and how it was used. A battery that fails unusually early in a well-maintained car is the kind of case they exist for.

They have limits. The Queensland Government notes that the guarantees do not cover [one-off private sales](https://www.qld.gov.au/law/your-rights/consumer-rights-complaints-and-scams/buying-products-and-services/guarantees-warranties-refunds/buying-at-private-sales-or-an-auction), so a car bought from a neighbour or an online classifieds seller is unlikely to qualify. If you bought from a dealer, put the claim to the dealer or the manufacturer in writing, with the service history and the diagnostic report attached.

## Your repair options, compared

Once no one else will pay, there are usually three ways to get the car running again.

- **A new pack from a franchised dealer.** Usually the most expensive and the most certain. You get new cells, a parts warranty and a fit done to the manufacturer's procedure.
- **A reconditioned or remanufactured pack.** A used pack that has been tested and had weak modules replaced. Quality varies between suppliers, so the warranty offered on the pack is the best guide to how much they trust it.
- **Module-level repair.** Only the failed modules are replaced. This can get an older car running again, but the rest of the pack is still original and ageing, so expect a shorter warranty, if there is one at all.

Whichever you are quoted, ask the same four questions: is the pack new, remanufactured or repaired; what warranty comes with it, in writing; who fits it; and what happens to the old pack. Getting quotes in writing lets you compare them with the car's value like for like.

## When selling makes more sense

A battery repair is worth doing when the car will be worth clearly more afterwards than the repair costs, and you intend to keep driving it. It is harder to justify when:

- the quote is a large share of what the car would be worth even with a working battery
- the car is already due for other significant work, or failed a safety inspection for something else
- you were planning to replace the car within a year or two anyway
- no repairer will put a meaningful warranty on the fix

A car with a dead hybrid battery still has value. How much depends on the make, model and year, the condition of the body and interior, whether the car is complete, and whether its parts are in demand. A pack left untouched in the car is also worth more to a dismantler than one that has been partly stripped or experimented on. Our guide to [what a scrap car is worth](/how-much-is-my-scrap-car-worth-gold-coast) explains what a buyer is actually paying for.

## Selling a hybrid that will not drive in Queensland

The registration status decides the paperwork. A registered car sold privately in Queensland generally needs a current safety certificate before it changes hands, and a car that will not go into READY mode is difficult to get inspected. That leaves two common paths:

- **Cancel the registration and sell it unregistered.** An [unregistered vehicle can be sold without a safety certificate](https://www.qld.gov.au/transport/buying/unregistered/selling). Our checklist for [cancelling Queensland registration](/cancel-car-registration-queensland-after-sale) covers the plates and any refund.
- **Trade it to a licensed motor dealer.** A trade to a licensed dealer does not need a safety certificate. Check that the licence belongs to the business actually buying the car; the [roadworthy guide](/sell-car-without-roadworthy-qld) explains why that matters.

Whoever buys it, be plain about the fault on the sale receipt, for example "hybrid system fault, sold as is, not driveable", with the date, the VIN and both signatures. It protects you if the buyer later says they were not told.

## Moving a hybrid that will not start

Do not push or tow a hybrid any distance with its drive wheels on the road until you have checked the owner's manual. Many hybrid manuals require the car to be moved with the drive wheels lifted, or with all four wheels off the ground on a flatbed, because turning the wheels can damage the drivetrain or generate electricity in the motor.

Tell whoever is collecting it that it is a hybrid, whether the steering is locked and where it is parked. A car stuck in an apartment basement in Surfers Paradise or Broadbeach needs the clearance height known in advance, because not every tilt-tray fits under a car-park ceiling. A car on a steep Currumbin Valley or Tallebudgera driveway needs different gear from one at a flat kerbside in Labrador.

## What happens to the old battery

A hybrid traction battery must not go in household rubbish or a kerbside bin. If the pack is replaced, the workshop doing the job should take the old one.

Toyota runs a [hybrid battery recycling program](https://www.toyota.com.au/explore/electrified/hybrid-electric/battery-recycling) through its dealers, which recover Toyota hybrid batteries for recycling. For other makes, ask the franchised dealer how they handle returned packs.

If the whole car is being sold for dismantling, leave the pack installed and the car complete. The pack then goes through the dismantler with the rest of the vehicle, and you are not left storing a heavy high-voltage battery in the garage.
